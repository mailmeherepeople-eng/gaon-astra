# Make players want one more village day

The strongest pitch is **a village life game where your neighbours remember you**. The reward for playing is a more interesting village: new people, funny incidents, beautiful places, useful tools and changes you can point to. Civics becomes the explanation for why the village responds.

These proposals come from the supplied `game-mechanics-catalogue.html`. Numbers below refer to its actual entries. This file is a roadmap; the September polish pass now implements save/resume, a relaxed opening, five physical neighbour favours, visible carrying, trust leading to an election, friendly characters and coordinated animals. The larger recurring stories and events below remain proposals.

## First, fix the foundations of fun

Save/resume and a relaxed villager opening are now implemented. A player-selectable pace setting remains an option. A player should not lose a beloved village by closing a tab, or abandon exploration because a two-minute day is running out. Keep costs and consequences, but make recovery possible. Replace repeated explanation popups with brief reactions, visible changes and optional journal entries.

Observe five to eight students playing the first ten minutes without instructions. Watch where they walk, what they try to touch, when they stop reading and whether they voluntarily ask to keep playing. This is a proposed test, not research already conducted.

## Suggested next playable slice

**“Meera’s market morning,” 15–20 minutes.**

1. A calf wanders into the community garden. Guide it back using movement and feed; Meera thanks you by name.
2. She asks you to deliver two milk cans. The shortest route crosses a damaged lane; a longer one is safe. No question appears.
3. At the haat, the trader offers a poor price. Pool milk with another producer or take the lower immediate price. Show both consequences.
4. Back home, villagers discuss whether tomorrow’s shared funds should fix the lane or improve water supply. Earlier experiences inform the choice.
5. The next day, the chosen improvement changes the route or water queue. The journal quietly records the story and offers an optional explanation.

This combines a playful animal encounter, a physical task, a meaningful decision and a visible result. It is more likely to establish a compelling loop than implementing eight unrelated systems at once. Validate that prediction in playtests before scaling it.

## How learning should accumulate

The catalogue’s **16.3.19 Discovery Tour warning** is useful: sightseeing alone does not ensure recall. Use **16.1.1 Spaced repetition** through varied situations rather than repeating a lecture. A water problem, school crossing and broken road can each ask the player to choose who to approach. Their choice should produce an understandable response, and a later story should require applying the same idea elsewhere.

Keep conventional exam practice in the optional journal. For the main game, reward informed action and let mistakes create recoverable stories. Avoid making each interaction a compulsory micro-question, even though the catalogue includes that mechanic. Avoid real-world attendance streaks, fear of losing pets while offline and endless collection with no interesting choice.

## What to measure

- Fun: voluntary return, self-directed exploration, favourite NPC, remembered funny moment, and desire to show someone the village.
- Usability: time to first successful interaction, misunderstood prompts, navigation failures and skipped text.
- Learning: an unseen situation solved without hints, an explanation in the player’s own words, and recall after several days.
- Balance: whether players can recover from a bad choice without restarting or grinding.

The desired outcome is a player who says “I want to see what happens tomorrow,” then discovers they can explain the civic systems they have been using.
